In my [previous investigation](https://canaicure.me/blog/investigating-my-genetic-mutation-with-ai/), I used a combination of AI and computational biology tools to investigate a genetic variant in my **COL6A3** gene. My variant, **I1563N** (`NM_004369.4.4688T>A, p.Ile1563Asn`), was classified as a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**, meaning there wasn’t enough evidence to determine whether it was disease-causing.

That investigation revealed several interesting clues. The affected amino acid was highly conserved across species, **AlphaMissense** and **AlphaGenome** suggested potentially damaging effects, and structural analysis showed that my mutation replaces a buried hydrophobic amino acid with a polar one, potentially disrupting its local structure or stability.

But so far, I'd been examining my mutation largely in isolation.

This time, I wanted to understand the bigger picture: **how collagen VI is built, where my mutation sits within COL6A3, and what other variants in the same region might tell us**.

## A primer on Collagen VI

When we hear *collagen*, we tend to think of skin and elasticity. But collagen isn't just one protein. The human body contains 28 recognised types of collagen, each with different roles in tissues ranging from skin and tendons to cartilage, bones and muscles.

**Collagen VI** is one of these types. Unlike the long rope-like fibres formed by some other collagens, it forms a distinctive network of fine, bead-like fibres called microfibrils, which help support and connect cells to their surrounding tissues.

Collagen VI is primarily built from three protein chains-- **α1(VI), α2(VI) and α3(VI)**-- encoded by the genes **COL6A1, COL6A2 and COL6A3**. These chains assemble into a collagen VI molecule, then into dimers, then tetramers, which are released outside the cell and join together to form a microfibrillar network.

But what does this network actually do?

Our cells are surrounded by a meshwork of proteins and other molecules called the **extracellular matrix (ECM)**, which provides structural support and helps cells communicate with their surroundings.

In muscle, this network helps anchor muscle fibres to surrounding connective tissue, distribute forces during contraction, and protect cells from mechanical stress. Collagen VI helps connect these supporting structures and influences signals involved in cell health and survival.

This helps explain why mutations in collagen VI can cause muscular dystrophy, even though collagen VI isn't directly responsible for muscle contraction. And because it's also present in tissues such as skin, tendons and parts of the nervous system, its dysfunction can have wide-ranging effects beyond muscle weakness.

I guess you could say collagen VI goes more than skin deep! ;)

## Mapping the COL6A3 Protein

Now that I understood how collagen VI assembles and what it does, I wanted to dive deeper into the particular protein affected by my mutation.

Interestingly, only a relatively small portion of COL6A3 forms the familiar collagen triple helix. 
Much of its size comes from an unusually long N-terminal region made up of repeated globular modules called **von Willebrand factor A (VWA) domains**, which help proteins interact with other molecules. 
Unlike COL6A1 and COL6A2, which each contain just one N-terminal VWA domain, COL6A3 contains **ten**.

This architecture matters because mutations in different parts of COL6A3 can affect the protein in different ways.

One of the best-understood mechanisms behind collagen VI-related muscular dystrophy involves mutations in the triple-helical collagenous region, particularly [substitutions of glycine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4520221/), a small amino acid essential for the three collagen chains to join together correctly. 

But my mutation doesn't sit there. It sits in the **N3 VWA domain**.

Previous studies of the [N5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3919171/) and [N2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7476709/) domains suggest that mutations outside the triple helix can also disrupt collagen VI function, potentially by affecting how these domains fold, interact with other molecules, or contribute to protein assembly and secretion.

Although these studies don't establish what my N3 mutation does, they provide a reason to investigate this region more closely.

The variant map revealed several reported associations with Bethlem myopathy, alongside a less expected connection to early-onset dystonia. Two variants stood out:
* [**G1517S**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110855/) has been reported in the context of early-onset dystonia, a movement disorder involving involuntary muscle contractions. This was particularly interesting because it suggested a possible connection between COL6A3 and neurological presentations, although the evidence remains limited.  
* [**I1563M**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/500624/?term=%28COL6A3%5Bgene%5D+AND+%22Ile1563Met%22%29) was a particularly intriguing find:   
  **Another variant affecting the exact same amino-acid position as mine\!**   
  It was submitted to ClinVar in association with Bethlem myopathy but remains classified as a VUS. While my mutation **I1563N** replaces isoleucine (hydrophobic) with asparagine (polar), **I1563M** replaces it with methionine (also hydrophobic). This more conservative substitution might be better tolerated structurally, making it an especially interesting comparison to mine.

The remaining nearby variants had more limited clinical evidence, but together they provided a useful set of comparisons in terms of their reported clinical associations.

## Comparing the Variants Structurally

Having identified several variants in and around the N3 domain, I wanted to understand how their positions within the protein's three-dimensional structure compared.

In my [previous investigation](https://canaicure.me/blog/investigating-my-genetic-mutation-with-ai/), I used AlphaFold and ChimeraX to analyse the structural environment of my mutation. I extended the same analysis to the nearby variants, comparing their solvent accessibility, structural confidence and local secondary structure.

G1517, A1560 and I1563 are deeply buried within the protein, whereas positions like Q1553 and R1591 are much more exposed.

My position, **I1563**, sits in a tightly packed, largely hydrophobic region of the N3 domain. This makes I1563M an especially interesting comparison: although both variants affect the same position, they introduce amino acids with different chemical properties.

This raises an interesting question: **could my substitution destabilise the N3 domain more than the conservative I1563M substitution?** The structural analysis alone can't answer that, but it provides a testable hypothesis for my next investigation.

## Putting the Pieces Together
Overall, the variant map revealed reported associations with **Bethlem myopathy and early-onset dystonia**, although the evidence behind these associations varies considerably. 
One particularly intriguing find was **I1563M, a variant affecting the exact same amino-acid position as mine**, but involving a more conservative substitution. 
The structural comparison also highlighted how my mutation introduces a polar amino acid into a tightly packed, largely hydrophobic region of the protein.

While none of this establishes that my variant is pathogenic, what started as an attempt to locate my mutation within COL6A3 has given me a much better understanding of its clinical context, structural environment, and potential significance.
